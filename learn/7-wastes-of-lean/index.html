The 7 wastes of lean, with examples

The 7 wastes of lean (muda) are overproduction, waiting, transportation, over-processing, inventory, motion and defects. They come from the Toyota Production System. Each is an activity or a condition that uses time or money but creates no value for the customer. Some non-value-adding work is still necessary (safety checks, legal requirements), so the goal is to remove the unnecessary part while protecting safety and quality. Below is a simple example of each, in a factory and outside one.

The 7 wastes with examples

WasteFactory exampleOffice / service example
OverproductionBuilding 120 parts when the next step needs 100 todayPrinting 50 reports when 10 people read them
WaitingAn operator watching a machine that could run on its ownA form waiting three days for a signature
TransportationMoving parts to a warehouse and back to the lineEmailing a file through four people before it reaches the person who acts on it
Over-processingPolishing a hidden surface beyond what function or quality requiresRe-typing data already in another system
InventoryA pile of work in progress between two processesA backlog of tickets nobody is working on
MotionWalking ten steps to fetch a tool for every unitSearching shared drives for the latest version
DefectsScrap and reworkAn invoice sent with the wrong amount

An easy way to remember them is TIMWOOD: Transportation, Inventory, Motion, Waiting, Over-processing, Overproduction, Defects. Some lists add an eighth — unused skills of people.

Why overproduction is called the worst

Overproduction creates the others. Extra parts need to be moved (transportation), stored (inventory) and searched for (motion), and defects in them are found late. It also hides problems: when there is always stock, nobody notices that a machine is slow or a step is unstable. See overproduction waste examples.

How to find the 7 wastes in your own work

  1. Follow one item (a part, a document, an order) from start to finish.
  2. Write down every step and sort it into: value-adding, necessary but not value-adding, and pure waste.
  3. For each pure-waste step, ask which of the seven wastes it is, and remove those first.
  4. Use overproduction and waiting as starting points, then trace the cause on the floor. Check for unevenness and overburden (mura and muri) as well as breakdowns and long changeovers.

Questions

What are the 7 wastes of lean?

Overproduction, waiting, transportation, over-processing, inventory, motion and defects. They come from the Toyota Production System and are often remembered as TIMWOOD.

Which of the 7 wastes is the worst?

Overproduction is usually called the worst, because it creates the other wastes (inventory, transportation, motion) and hides problems.

Do the 7 wastes apply outside manufacturing?

Yes. Offices, hospitals and restaurants have the same wastes: waiting for approvals, re-typing data, backlogs, searching for files and errors that need rework.

What is the 8th waste?

Some lists add unused skills or talent of people. It is not in the original seven from Toyota, but it is widely used.
