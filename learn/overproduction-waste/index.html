Overproduction waste examples: factory, office and service, with numbers

Overproduction waste means making more than the customer needs, or making it earlier than needed. It is one of the seven wastes of the Toyota Production System, and it is often called the worst one, because it creates the other wastes and hides problems. Below are concrete overproduction waste examples — in a factory, in an office and in services — with numbers.

Example 1: a line that "never stops"

A plant receives an order of 480 units a day and has 480 minutes of working time, so the takt time is one minute per unit. The rule on the floor is "never let a process stop, build as much as you can," and the line builds 600 units a day.

  • 120 extra units are made every day.
  • If each unit ties up, say, $10 of material, that is $1,200 a day sitting on the floor.
  • If the extra units are not used later, after 20 working days that is 2,400 units and $24,000 — plus the space to store them and the people to move them.

On the books, inventory is an asset, so the plant can look profitable. On the floor, money is tied up in material and inventory, and the extra units need space, handling and checking.

Example 2: piles between processes

Process A runs faster than process B. Nobody has set how many parts may wait between them, so A keeps going. By the afternoon there are hundreds of parts in front of B. If B finds a defect that started in A, many of the parts waiting in the pile may have the same problem — and A may have been making it for hours.

Example 3: batch printing in an office

A team prints and assembles 500 information packs at the start of the month "to be efficient." Halfway through, the form changes. The remaining packs go in the recycling bin. The waste came from printing more packs than were needed, before they were needed.

Example 4: food prepared ahead in a restaurant

A kitchen preps 80 portions of a dish before lunch because "it was busy last Friday." Only 50 sell. The rest is thrown out at closing, and the cooks spent the morning on food nobody ordered instead of on what customers wanted.

Why overproduction is often called the worst waste

Overproduction leads to…How
InventoryWhat is made early usually has to be stored.
TransportationIt is often moved to storage and back again.
MotionPeople search through piles for the right item.
Defects found lateProblems hide in the pile until the next process uses it.
Hidden waitingSpare people and machines look busy, so nobody sees the extra capacity.

How to spot overproduction

  • Work-in-process regularly exceeds its agreed limit, or piles up between steps with no limit at all.
  • "We had time, so we made the next batch" is normal.
  • Machine or labor utilization is a target in itself.
  • Aisles and spare corners fill up with material.

How to reduce it

  1. Work to takt time. Set the pace from customer demand (available time ÷ demand), not from machine speed.
  2. Pull, don't push. Let the next process take what it needs, when it needs it, and make only what was taken. Kanban is a common tool for this.
  3. Set a WIP limit. Decide how much work-in-process may wait between steps — enough to keep the line flowing — and stop producing when the limit is reached. Review it as conditions change.
  4. Allow stopping. If there is nothing needed, stop. The idle time makes spare capacity visible, so it can be used for improvement or moved where it is needed.

Questions

What is overproduction waste in lean?

Making more than the customer needs, or making it earlier than needed. It is one of the seven wastes of the Toyota Production System.

What is a simple example of overproduction waste?

A line that has an order of 480 units a day but builds 600 because no process is allowed to stop. The 120 extra units become inventory that needs space, handling and money.

Why is overproduction considered the worst of the seven wastes?

Because it creates other wastes such as inventory, transportation and motion, and it hides problems such as defects and idle capacity.

Does overproduction happen outside manufacturing?

Yes. Printing documents before they are needed, preparing more food than is likely to sell and processing batches before the next step needs them can all be overproduction.
