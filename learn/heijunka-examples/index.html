Heijunka examples: production leveling in real life, with numbers

Heijunka means leveling both the volume and the mix of what you build, day by day and hour by hour. Instead of running one model for days and then switching, you build a little of each model every day. It is one of the foundations of the Toyota Production System — just-in-time and kanban assume it is in place. Below are concrete heijunka examples with numbers.

Example 1: a week of production, batched vs. leveled

A plant builds 100 units a week over 5 days: 60 of model A, 20 of B and 20 of C.

MonTueWedThuFri
BatchedA 20A 20A 20B 20C 20
Leveled (heijunka)A12 B4 C4A12 B4 C4A12 B4 C4A12 B4 C4A12 B4 C4

Both plans build 20 units a day. Only the mix changes. Within the day, the leveled plan repeats the sequence A B A C A four times. This is mix leveling.

Volume leveling is the other half. Even if orders arrive as 40, 20, 10, 20 and 10 units across the week, you still plan 20 a day and cover Monday's shortfall with finished-goods stock or adjusted delivery dates.

Example 2: what the supplier sees

Seen from the final assembly line, batching looks easier: only two changeovers a week. Now look at a supplier that makes a part used only on model B.

  • Batched: it needs 20 sets on Thursday and zero on the other days. To be ready, it must either keep enough people and machines to make 20 sets in one day, or build them ahead by Thursday and hold the stock.
  • Leveled: it needs 4 sets every day. Daily capacity for 4 sets is enough — one fifth of the batched peak — and much less stock is built ahead.

Batching pushes cost upstream — capacity for the peak or inventory — and that cost comes back in the part price. Toyota explains it as mura (unevenness) creating muri (overburden), which creates muda (waste: idle people and machines, inventory). Heijunka reduces the mura in the production plan itself.

Example 3: the heijunka box

A heijunka box (leveling box) is a rack of slots: one column per time interval and one row per model. In Example 1, 20 units in an 8-hour day means one unit every 24 minutes. Use 24-minute columns and one card per unit, and place the cards in the A, B, A, C, A rows across five columns, repeated four times — 12 A, 4 B and 4 C a day. A material handler collects one column every 24 minutes and delivers it to the process, so work arrives at a steady pace instead of in waves. (In practice a slot often holds several cards, one per container.)

Example 4: outside the factory

  • A bakery that bakes every bread type in small rounds through the morning, instead of all baguettes at 6 a.m., can spread the load on ovens and staff and restock shelves more often.
  • An office team that processes a few of every request type each day, instead of all invoices on Monday, can spread its workload more evenly through the week.

These are simplified illustrations, but the principle is the same: smooth the mix so the people and resources behind you see a more even load.

Common misunderstandings

  1. It is not leveling customer demand. You cannot remove the waves in orders. You level your build sequence and absorb the waves with finished-goods stock, adjusted lead times or both.
  2. It needs short changeovers. Building every model every day means more changeovers. Reducing changeover time (SMED) and heijunka go together.
  3. Volume leveling and mix leveling are different. If total monthly volume changes a lot, revisit the daily production rate and the takt time (available time divided by required units). Leveling the mix alone cannot absorb that.

Questions

What is a simple heijunka example?

A plant that builds 60 A, 20 B and 20 C in a week can run A for three days, then B, then C — or build 12 A, 4 B and 4 C every day in the sequence A B A C A. The second plan is heijunka.

What is the difference between heijunka and leveling?

They are the same idea. Heijunka is the Japanese term used in the Toyota Production System for leveling production volume and mix.

What does a heijunka box do?

It holds kanban cards in slots by time interval and model, in the leveled order, so work is released to the line at a steady pace.

How is heijunka related to mura, muri and muda?

Uneven schedules (mura) overburden people and machines (muri), which leads to waste such as idle capacity and inventory (muda). Heijunka reduces that unevenness in the plan itself.
