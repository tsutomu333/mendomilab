Mura, muri, muda: meaning and examples

Mura, muri and muda are three related ideas in the Toyota Production System, often called the "3 Ms": mura is unevenness, muri is overburden, and muda is waste — work that adds no value. They are connected — unevenness tends to create overburden, and overburden leads to waste. Below are concrete examples, and why improvement usually starts with mura.

On Japanese shop floors the trio is often remembered as "darari" — the last sounds of muda, mura and muri — or simply as the 3 Mu. Either way, people check all three together.

The three Ms at a glance

MeaningTypical sign
Mura (斑)Unevenness in volume, mix or timingBusy days and quiet days; end-of-month rush
Muri (無理)Overburden on people or machinesOvertime, rushing, skipped checks, breakdowns
Muda (無駄)Activity that adds no value for the customerInventory, waiting, extra transport, rework

Example 1: a factory with a batched schedule

A plant builds 100 units a week: model A Monday to Wednesday, B on Thursday, C on Friday. A supplier that makes a part only for B sees zero demand four days a week and 20 sets on Thursday (mura). Assume the people and machine on this part have little other work. On Thursday they work overtime and the machine runs flat out (muri). The rest of the week, the same people and machines sit idle — or keep building stock ahead (muda). If the plant builds 4 B units every day and the supplier ships to match, the day-to-day mura in this example disappears, and the muri and much of the muda go with it. See heijunka examples.

Example 2: an office at month end

Invoices arrive all month but are processed in the last three days (mura). The team works late and makes mistakes under pressure (muri). Mistakes are reworked and customers wait (muda). Processing a few invoices every day evens out the load.

Example 3: a restaurant at lunch

Everyone arrives between 12:00 and 12:30 (mura). The kitchen is overloaded and orders back up (muri). Guests leave without eating, and staff stand idle at 14:00 (muda). Taking reservations at staggered times or preparing some items before the peak spreads the load.

Why start with mura?

Many teams attack muda first — cutting inventory, chasing idle time. But if the unevenness stays, the waste comes back: the inventory was there to absorb the waves. Leveling the work (heijunka) and setting standard work so no one is overloaded make the remaining waste easier to see and remove. This is a priority, not a strict order: obvious overburden that risks safety or quality should be fixed right away.

Common mistakes

  • Treating muda as the only target. Removing buffers without removing the unevenness usually creates muri.
  • Calling everything "waste." Some buffer is a deliberate choice while the unevenness still exists. Decide it on purpose and shrink it as you improve.
  • Ignoring muri. Overburdened people and machines lead to errors, injuries and breakdowns — which become more muda.

Questions

What do mura, muri and muda mean?

Mura is unevenness, muri is overburden, and muda is activity that adds no value for the customer. Together they are the three Ms of the Toyota Production System.

What is an example of mura, muri and muda?

A batched schedule gives a supplier 20 sets of demand on one day and none on the others (mura). That day its people work overtime (muri). On the other days they sit idle or build stock ahead (muda).

Which should you eliminate first?

Usually mura. Leveling the work removes the cause of much of the overburden and waste. Removing waste while the unevenness remains tends to bring the waste back or create overburden.

How is heijunka related to mura?

Heijunka (production leveling) is the main way to reduce mura in the production plan, by smoothing volume and mix over time.
