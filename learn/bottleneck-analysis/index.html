Bottleneck analysis in lean: how to find and fix a bottleneck

A bottleneck is the step with the lowest capacity in a process. It sets the maximum output of the whole line. However fast the other steps are, the system cannot produce more than the bottleneck. That is why improvement starts with finding it. Below is a simple bottleneck analysis with numbers.

A bottleneck example with numbers

Four steps run in sequence. Their hourly capacity is:

StepABCD
Units per hour60405070

Even with plenty of orders, the line can produce at most 40 units an hour, because B is the bottleneck. Adding people to A so it makes 80 an hour changes nothing — the parts simply pile up in front of B.

Improve B to 60 an hour and the limit rises to 50. Now C is the bottleneck. Fix one bottleneck and another step — or demand itself — becomes the next constraint.

How to calculate capacity

Daily capacity of a step = available time per day ÷ cycle time of that step

With 480 minutes (28,800 seconds) available and a 72-second cycle time, a step can make 28,800 ÷ 72 = 400 units a day. If demand is 480 a day (a 60-second takt time), that step cannot keep up. Steps whose cycle time is longer than takt time lack capacity for demand; if there are several, the slowest is the bottleneck candidate. This is an estimate that ignores downtime and defects. See takt time vs cycle time.

Four signs of a bottleneck on the floor

  • Work piles up in front of it.
  • The next step is waiting for it.
  • It never stops, while other steps have idle time.
  • Its cycle time is longer than takt time when you measure it.

Don't be fooled by how busy a place looks. The most hectic spot is not always the bottleneck, and a queue can be caused somewhere else.

How to fix a bottleneck, step by step

  1. Find it with the signs above and the numbers.
  2. Never let it stop. Stagger breaks, shorten changeovers, and keep a small buffer in front of it so no bottleneck time is wasted.
  3. Pace everything else to it. Steps faster than the bottleneck only build inventory if they keep running.
  4. Raise its capacity. Remove waste from the work, or add people or equipment there.
  5. Look for the next one. The bottleneck moves, so go back to step 1.

This sequence follows the same logic as the five focusing steps of the theory of constraints.

Common mistakes

  • "More people means more output." Adding people anywhere other than the bottleneck adds cost, not output.
  • "Every step should run at full speed." Running non-bottlenecks flat out is overproduction (overproduction waste examples).
  • "Bottlenecks are machines." Manual work, inspection, transport and office approvals can all be bottlenecks.

Bottlenecks outside the factory

In a restaurant, the bottleneck can be the seats, the kitchen, the servers, the cashier or the dishwashing. Empty tables but slow food: the kitchen. Food ready but not served: the floor staff. Finished guests queuing to pay: the cashier. Adding staff everywhere because it is busy can raise cost without raising sales.

Questions

What is a bottleneck in lean?

The step with the lowest capacity in a process. It limits the output of the whole system.

How do you calculate a bottleneck?

Divide available time by each step's cycle time to get its capacity. The step with the lowest capacity is the bottleneck. 480 minutes (28,800 seconds) at a 72-second cycle time gives 28,800 ÷ 72 = 400 units a day, before downtime and defects.

How do you identify a bottleneck on the floor?

Work piles up in front of it, the next step waits for it, it never stops while others have idle time, and its cycle time is longer than takt time.

What happens after you fix a bottleneck?

Another step, or demand itself, becomes the new constraint. Bottleneck analysis is a cycle: find it, fix it, and look for the next one.
