Stalled Factory, Paused Factory: a game on availability vs. utilization

If you track machine time with one number, a breakdown and a planned stop both look like "not running." The Toyota Production System keeps them apart: availability asks whether the machine could run when it was needed, and utilization asks how much it ran against the order. In this game you choose how many machines to own, run the day, and see the difference in numbers and in money.

What participants learn

  • Availability (bekidō-ritsu, 可動率): the share of the needed time a machine could actually run. Breakdown time (red) is something to fix. The target is 100%.
  • Utilization (kasedō-ritsu, 稼働率): how much a machine ran against its capacity. The order decides it, so a low number can be fine.
  • Why red makes you own extra machines to win back the lost time.
  • Why running machines to fill idle time (yellow) is overproduction.
  • When there is a lot of yellow, the real question is whether you own too many machines.

Who it is for and how long it takes

New hires in manufacturing, maintenance and production planning teams, and operations management classes. 15–20 minutes alone. In a workshop, 30–45 minutes; it pairs well with Overproduction Factory.

How a session runs

  1. Stage 1 (red): in a plant with breakdowns, find how many machines meet the order. On paper 3 should be enough — why are they not?
  2. Debrief: the facilitator asks: "Can the red time be removed? If it were, how many machines would you need?"
  3. Stage 2 (yellow): in a plant with no breakdowns, head office says "raise utilization" on a slow day. What do you do?
  4. Back to your floor: do your own numbers keep red and yellow apart?

The same word, different denominators

"Utilization" and "availability" are defined differently from company to company and field to field. In English the confusion is worse, because OEE uses "availability" for something close to what Toyota calls bekidō-ritsu. This game uses the TPS definitions.

DefinitionDenominatorBreakdown time
TPS utilization (kasedō-ritsu)Capacity in the planned shiftNot removed. It shows up in availability instead
Utilization with downtime removedTime the machine could run (shift − breakdowns etc.)Disappears from the number

Why TPS does not remove breakdown time from utilization

If breakdown time is taken out of the denominator, a breakdown no longer makes the number worse. Take an 8-hour shift, a machine that makes 50 parts an hour, and an order of 300. Breakdowns take 3 hours and only 250 parts are made.

DenominatorCalculationResult
Planned shift (TPS)250 ÷ 40062.5%
Shift minus breakdowns250 ÷ (5 h × 50)100%

With breakdowns removed, the day reads 100% even though the order is 50 parts short. The problem disappears from the numbers. So TPS keeps two numbers: utilization uses the planned shift and asks whether output matches demand, and availability shows the time lost to breakdowns and changeovers. Neither can hide the other.

When someone quotes a utilization figure, ask first: "What is the denominator?" It saves a lot of talking past each other.

Questions

What is the difference between availability and utilization?

Availability looks only at time lost to breakdowns and similar problems: of the time the machine was needed, how much could it run? Utilization compares running time with full capacity, so it also counts time stopped because there was no order. Availability aims for 100%; utilization depends on demand.

Why does Toyota read the two words differently?

In Japanese both 可動率 and 稼働率 can be read "kadō-ritsu." Toyota reads the first as "bekidō-ritsu" and the second as "kasedō-ritsu" so people can tell them apart by ear.

Is this the same as OEE?

No. OEE multiplies availability, performance and quality into one number. This game is about a different point: keeping breakdown stops and planned stops apart, because one should be fixed and the other is fine.

Why doesn't TPS take breakdown time out of the utilization denominator?

Because then breakdowns would stop lowering the number. A day that is 50 parts short of a 300-part order can still read 100% if the lost hours are removed from the denominator. TPS keeps utilization and availability apart so breakdowns cannot hide.
